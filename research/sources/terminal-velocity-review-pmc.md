## Abstract

An expression was developed for prediction of drag coefficients for any spherical particle, drop or bubble in an infinite, homogeneous liquid. The formula reproduces the limiting cases for gas bubbles and solid spheres, as well as the exact Hadamard-Rybczynski solution. The accuracy of the expression, which is valid for Reynolds numbers up to a few hundred, was confirmed by comparison with published numerical predictions of the drag coefficient for a range of physical circumstances.

## Introduction

Bubbles, drops and particles are widespread in science and engineering phenomena. Knowledge of the behavior of single bubbles and drops is not only directly relevant to many applications; it also supports understanding of the corresponding swarms, e.g., \[ [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref001)\].

Wegener et al. \[ [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref002)\] recently provided a comprehensive review of theory, experimental data and pertinent approximations describing the dynamics of single drops in fluid systems. The steady rate of movement of spherical particle, drops and bubbles is quantified by the drag coefficient, _C_ _d_. Of relevance here is Wegener et al.’s summary of drag formulas (their Tables [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.t001) and [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.t002)), and the ranges of (drop) Reynolds numbers over which the different formulas apply.

We consider the drag on a single spherical solid particle, liquid drop or gas bubble (collectively referred to as a particle) moving in an otherwise quiescent, infinite, homogeneous liquid, without interphase mass transfer (i.e., Sherwood number of zero). Spherical particles will occur if surface tension is dominant, or if inertia is negligible. Clift et al. \[ [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref003)\] and Bhaga and Weber \[ [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref004)\] provide systematic characterizations of particle shape as it varies with Reynolds, Eötvös and Morton numbers. These authors show, for instance, that spherical shapes for Reynolds numbers of about 100 or higher are found for sufficiently small Eötvös and Morton numbers.

Below, we develop and test a new expression for _C_ _d_ applicable to spherical particles. Our approach is to build an interpolation based on known limiting cases (e.g., small Reynolds number), as well as a validated large Reynolds number drag expression.

### Analytical drag results for large Reynolds number

Unlike the situation for _R_ 0 ≪ 1, exact results for _R_ 0 ≫ 1 do not exist. For laminar flow (i.e., oscillations of the particle do not occur), Harper and Moore \[ [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref013)\] as well as Parlange \[ [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref014)\] obtained approximate expressions for the drag for this case, however. In both \[ [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref013)\] and \[ [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref014)\], it was observed that, to a first approximation, flow inside the particle is described by a Hill’s vortex, and outside by a potential flow. Both approaches give drag predictions that are “numerically indistinguishable” \[ [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref014)\]. This conclusion follows from the minor effect on the drag made by slightly different assumptions in \[ [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref013)\] and \[ [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref014)\]. Barry and Parlange \[ [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref015)\] compared predictions of both theories to experimental results on recirculation within the particle \[ [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref004)\], and found that the theory of Parlange \[ [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref014)\] is more accurate. Thus, this theory is the starting point for the developments presented below.

## Conclusion

We developed a formula, [Eq (10)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.e011), to predict the drag for a spherical particle, for all viscosity ratios between gas bubbles and solid spheres. It was derived as a Padé approximant that interpolates between known analytical results at low and moderate Reynolds numbers assuming that the particle does not oscillate or wobble. The formula can be used to predict the drag coefficient for spherical bubbles, drops and particles for any viscosity and density ratios. Surface tension is assumed sufficient to maintain the spherical shape of the particle. Both [Fig 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.g002).5 of Clift et al. \[ [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref003)\] and Fig \[ [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref008)\] of Bhaga and Weber \[ [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref004)\] show how the particle shape changes (spherical, ellipsoidal, spherical cap, etc.) with the Eötvös, Morton and Reynolds numbers, and thus indicate the range of conditions for which [Eq (10)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.e011) applies. [Eq (10)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.e011) is more theoretically based than, for instance, the formula of Rivkind and Ryskin \[ [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901779/#pone.0194907.ref009)\] and appears to be more accurate as well, especially at low Reynolds numbers.
