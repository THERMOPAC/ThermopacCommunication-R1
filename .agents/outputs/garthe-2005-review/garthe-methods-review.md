SCOPE/OBJECTIVES
- The dissertation targets scale-up/design of counter-current liquid-liquid extraction columns, where drop size/distribution, breakage/coalescence, internals, energy input, swarm/hold-up, and mass transfer are coupled and incompletely understood (PDF pp. 1–3; lines 300–325).
- Explicit objectives: investigate single rigid spheres, single drops, and particle/drop swarms; quantify effects of drop/particle size, energy input and internals on velocity, breakage and mass transfer; quantify concentration/hold-up effects; provide inputs for drop-population-balance models (DPBMs) (p. 3; lines 363–377).
- Systems/columns explicitly covered: polypropylene spheres and two EFCE standard liquid systems (toluene/acetone/water and butyl acetate/acetone/water), in pulsed sieve-tray and structured-packed columns and agitated rotating-disc (RDC) and Kühni columns (p. 4; lines 382–387; pp. 49–53, lines 2661–2677, 2717–2727).
- Design premise is not a claim that single-drop tests alone are universally sufficient: the text says preliminary/pilot tests cannot currently be abandoned for reliable dimensioning (p. 2; lines 329–359; summary p. 149, lines 7947–7955).

EXPERIMENTAL SYSTEMS/APParATUS AND METHODS
- Single rigid-sphere mini-plant: 80-mm ID, 1-m height, ~10 L, pulsed or agitated, water held at 20 °C; spheres pumped to bottom then pump stopped to create stationary water; velocity measured over 300 mm by 25-frame/s camcorder; >=50 measurements per size/mode; pulsation stroke 8 mm, agitator 0–2000 rpm (pp. 54–55; lines 2891–2916).
- Single-drop mini-plant: syringe/steel-capillary generation and 8-way valve; nominal drop diameter within 1%; stationary 20 °C water; >=50 velocity measurements. Breakage used single tray, single packing, or one/two agitators; daughter number visually/camera assessed; daughter sizes/distributions measured with Pilhofer photoelectric suction probe (pp. 55–57; lines 2973–2985, 3051–3089).
- Probe principle: drops are cylindrically deformed in a glass capillary and optical transit/length converts to volume-equivalent spherical diameter; 1,000 drops measured per experiment. Mass transfer coefficient βod obtained from concentration change over a defined 200-mm measuring section, with drop concentration measured before/after internals and continuous-phase samples at two locations (pp. 56–57; lines 3064–3101).
- Rigid-sphere swarm pilot column: 3.65-m high, 80-mm ID; recirculating particle/water tank, separate water counterflow and particle recirculation; flows weighed/volumetrically measured; steady state required for one hour. Hold-up from bed height after pneumatic slide-valve closure, with dense-bed fraction calibration (pp. 58–60; lines 3150–3258).
- Drop-swarm pilot extractor: 80-mm ID and ~4.4-m overall height; 1-mm-hole finger distributor (4x12); organic phase dispersed below active section, aqueous phase counter-current; sieve trays, structured packings and agitators tested; flow meters/pump calibrations and temperature monitoring at 20 °C. Drop-size probes at inlet plus three axial positions (>=1,000 drops each); hold-up from three simultaneous 300-mm valve sections; concentration samples at inlets/outlets and three internal positions using selective porous filters (pp. 60–63; lines 3262–3420).
- Fluids/control: mutually saturated phases for fluid-dynamics tests; for mass-transfer tests water and solvent mutually saturated before acetone addition to avoid superimposed mass-transfer currents. Deionized water conductivity ~0.63 µS/cm at 20 °C; lab-grade liquids replaced if settling/coalescence changed or continuous phase stood three weeks; pilot liquids technically pure and regenerated by distillation (pp. 52–53; lines 2817–2839, 2870–2874).

DATASETS AND MODELLING
- Sphere datasets span diameters roughly 1.9–4.0 mm (single) and 3.0–4.0 mm (monodisperse swarms), with density/size checked by random examination of 500 particles per size (p. 50; lines 2685–2713).
- Drop swarm experiments used ~5 wt-% acetone aqueous feed, solute-free organic feed, fixed vd/vc=1.2 and stepped total throughput toward flooding; measured Sauter diameter, hold-up and concentration profiles in three sections (pp. 135–136; lines 7258–7274). Appendix tables contain extensive raw/profile data (e.g., Tables A.15–A.20, PDF pp. 229–258; lines 12005–13765).
- The new swarm model derives swarm influence from single-particle characteristic velocity and drag-vs-Reynolds behavior (eq. 8.3; pp. 125–132; lines 6705–6724, 6966–7048, 7078–7113). It is intended to feed DPBMs, which discretize drop-size classes along column height (p. 2; lines 354–359).

VALIDATION AND QUANTITATIVE RESULTS
- Validation compares predicted/measured hold-up, using measured single-particle terminal/characteristic velocities; hold-up is preferred because it is easier to measure than swarm velocity (p. 133; lines 7193–7207).
- Rigid spheres: 145 points, mean relative error 18.7%, across no internals, Montz-Pak B1-350, RDC and Kühni; pp. 3–4 spheres, pulsation 1–2 cm/s and rpm 200–400 (p. 134; lines 7209–7246). Sieve-tray sphere tests failed because 4-mm holes still flooded (p. 134; lines 7248–7252)—an important missing validation domain.
- Pulsed sieve tray: own 2-mm-hole tests at 1 and 2 cm/s plus literature; 276 points, 18.1% relative error, broadly good for continuous-to-dispersed transfer/no transfer across wide geometry/throughput range (pp. 135–136; lines 7288–7338).
- Structured packing: 227 points, 22.9% error; satisfactory at 1–2 cm/s and literature range B=10–80 m³/(m² h) (p. 137; lines 7357–7408).
- RDC: 244 points, 18.5% error; generally good, including literature rpm up to 900, although the underlying single-drop velocity correlation was validated only to 400 rpm (pp. 138–139; lines 7416–7465). Drops with Sauter diameter >4.5 mm cannot be satisfactorily predicted (p. 139; lines 7472–7476).
- Kühni: 118 points, 23.5% average relative error; larger errors at 20% free stator area, better agreement at larger free areas; pilot operating constraints caused butyl-acetate tests above 150 rpm to flood (p. 139; lines 7480–7523).
- External project validation: dissertation says partner DPBMs were validated for fluid dynamics and mass transfer, but directs detailed evidence to partners/Final Report AiF 40 ZN 2004 rather than presenting it fully here (p. 140; lines 7537–7546; p. 4, lines 394–405). Treat that as secondary/indirect validation, not independently reproducible evidence in this dissertation.

MAJOR CONCLUSIONS
- Characteristic velocity is mainly controlled by particle/drop size and internal geometry; Kühni rotational speed is additionally important. Breakage and mass transfer depend strongly on fluid properties, size and energy input (summary p. 149; lines 7957–7965).
- Correlations for velocity, breakage probability, daughter number and daughter-size distribution agree with own/literature data and reduce the number of single-drop experiments needed (p. 149; lines 7967–7975).
- New swarm model claims accurate hold-up prediction across internals and allows swarm influence to be estimated from single-particle data (pp. 139–140; lines 7528–7533; summary p. 150, lines 7983–7989).
- Montz-Pak B1-350 structured packing combines high mass-transfer rate and throughput; RDC gives very low mass transfer due to low disc energy input and low breakage (p. 150; lines 7991–7995).
- Energy input improves transfer but also increases breakage, creates smaller drops, and can cause flooding; therefore “as high as possible” is explicitly limited by flooding/throughput constraints (p. 124; lines 6657–6666).

LIMITATIONS / EXPLICIT CAUTIONS
- Richardson–Zaki is validated only for rigid spheres in unhindered columns; applying it to drops or internals is explicitly questionable (p. 125; lines 6682–6701). Structured-pack swarm exponents can be 0.95–2.8, below Richardson–Zaki’s minimum 2.39 (pp. 128–129; lines 6886–6924).
- Model deviations are materially directional: d-to-c transfer generates many coalescence events and large, nonspherical drops (>~4 mm) whose characteristic velocities are not captured; optimization for this direction was deemed unnecessary because technical processes usually suppress coalescence via transfer-direction choice (p. 136; lines 7340–7350; p. 137, lines 7404–7411).
- Coalescence and surfactant/impurity effects remain uncertain and can change behavior significantly; axial mixing is difficult and under-investigated. Authors explicitly recommend other liquid systems, extensive coalescence and axial-mixing work (p. 150; lines 8002–8009; literature discussion pp. 47–48, lines 2602–2637).
- Findings are strongly bounded by two standard systems, 20 °C, specified internals and operating ranges, mostly c-to-d mass transfer, and measured-size/hold-up validation rather than universal mass-transfer validation. The dissertation’s own framing retains pilot/preliminary testing as necessary for reliable design (pp. 2–3; lines 329–359; p. 150; lines 7947–7955).
